## _Climate Conspiracies: Stratospheric Aerosol Injections and the Chemtrails Conspiracy_
### Introduction
Have you ever looked up at the sky and seen a line of clouds trailing an airplane? What you’re looking at is condensed airplane exhaust, called a contrail (Heymsfield 2003). It is believed by some that these are actually “chemtrails” and are part of a covert operation by the government to spray chemicals into the sky for weather and mind control (Tingley and Wagner 2017). While chemtrails may be a conspiracy, the concept of releasing particles into the atmosphere isn’t fictional. Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) is a proposed geoengineering technique, involving the deliberate large-scale modification of Earth's climate, aiming to combat global warming by releasing sulphur particles into the atmosphere to reflect radiation and cool the Earth. However, in many facets of social media, the term “geoengineering” is synonymous with the chemtrails conspiracy, blurring the line between fact and fiction. How might these social media narratives impact the implementation and policy of SAI strategies?

### A background on stratospheric aerosol injections
Stratospheric aerosol injections would cool the Earth by increasing the amount of solar radiation the atmosphere reflects, mimicking the cooling mechanisms of sulfur particles released by volcanic eruptions. To do this, SAI augments Earth’s natural layer of tiny sulfur-containing particles in the lower stratosphere, by injecting additional sulfur particles into it (Figure 1) (Weisenstein et al. 2022). The particles scatter and reflect incoming short-wave solar radiation back into space before it reaches Earth’s surface, thereby cooling the climate and providing time for long-term mitigation and adaptation strategies to be implemented (Weisenstein et al. 2022). Although scientific modeling of SAI encourages its cooling ability, this technology has yet to be implemented due to challenges including alteration of natural geochemical cycles and international governmental agreement (Miller 2026; Niemeier and Timmreck 2015; Hunyh and McNeill 2024).

### Conspiracy theories and their association with SAI
This is where the chemtrails conspiracy comes back into play. Some conspiracy theorists argue that if the government is openly proposing releasing chemicals through SAI, then they may be employing this technology without the public’s knowledge to control the weather—or worse, the population. Yet, there exists no credible evidence proving that contrails, or other atmospheric modification is being used for weather or population control. Despite this, between 2008 and 2017, approximately 60% of social media discourse regarding geoengineering was conspiracy related, and 30–40% of Americans surveyed considered conspiracies about chemtrails to be somewhat true (Tingley and Wagner 2017). 

The harm is, these beliefs don’t just stay on social media, they can also enter political discussions. In 2024, a Tennessee bill was passed (Senate Bill 2691) that enacted restrictions on intentional atmospheric releases that were “intended to affect temperature, weather, or sunlight”, where chemtrail related claims appeared in the bill's lawmaking (“Bill Information - Tennessee General Assembly,” n.d.). 

### Outlook of Stratospheric Aerosol Injections and Public Perception 
Ultimately, the chemtrails conspiracy circulates misinformation which, through social media and online discourse, is now tied to geoengineering as a whole. As scientists continue to develop advanced ways to combat climate change, like the proposal of stratospheric aerosol injections, clear and accurate scientific communication for the public eye will be more important than ever.
